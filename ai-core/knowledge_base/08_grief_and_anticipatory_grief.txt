TOPIC: Grief and anticipatory grief
SOURCE: Based on general guidance from NCI and WHO cancer support resources

Grief in cancer isn't limited to end-of-life situations — patients often grieve losses throughout treatment: the loss of a former routine, physical ability, fertility, career plans, or a sense of invulnerability. This grief is valid regardless of prognosis, and naming it as grief, rather than just "being upset," can help it feel less confusing.

For patients facing advanced or terminal diagnoses, anticipatory grief — grieving a loss that hasn't fully happened yet — is common among both patients and their families. It can involve waves of sadness alongside ordinary daily life, which can feel disorienting, as if you should be either fully grieving or fully normal, when in reality both can happen in the same day.

There's no fixed timeline or "correct" way to move through this grief. Some people want to talk about it openly, including practical matters like end-of-life planning, while others prefer to focus on the present and avoid the topic — both are legitimate coping styles, though they can create friction if a patient and family member cope differently.

Support specifically for anticipatory grief exists through palliative care teams, hospital chaplains or spiritual care (regardless of religious affiliation), and grief counselors, and reaching out doesn't require waiting until a crisis point.
