TOPIC: Talking to family and children about your diagnosis
SOURCE: Based on general guidance from NCI and WHO cancer support resources

Deciding what to share, and with whom, is one of the harder early decisions after a diagnosis. There's no single right answer — some patients want to tell everyone immediately for support, others prefer to keep the circle small at first. Both approaches are valid, and the choice can also change over time as treatment progresses.

With children specifically, research generally supports being honest in age-appropriate terms rather than hiding the diagnosis entirely. Children often sense that something is wrong even when adults try to shield them, and unexplained tension can be more frightening than a clear, simple explanation. For younger children, this might mean naming the illness and reassuring them about who will take care of them day to day. For teenagers, more detail and more room for their own questions and reactions is usually appropriate.

With partners and close family, one common challenge is that everyone processes the news on a different timeline — a patient may feel ready to talk practically about treatment while a partner is still in shock, or vice versa. Naming this mismatch out loud ("I know we're each dealing with this differently right now") can reduce friction from feeling out of sync.

It can also help to designate one person to update extended family and friends, rather than repeating the same conversation many times, which can be emotionally draining. Support groups or a social worker on your care team can offer specific scripts or guidance for harder conversations, like talking to young children or discussing prognosis.
