TOPIC: Body image changes during and after treatment
SOURCE: Based on general guidance from NCI and WHO cancer support resources

Cancer treatment can change the body in visible ways — hair loss, surgical scars, weight changes, or the presence of medical devices — and these changes can affect self-image in ways that catch people off guard, even when they intellectually understood treatment side effects beforehand. Seeing yourself change in the mirror is a different experience than being warned about it in advance.

These reactions are common and don't mean you're being vain or ungrateful for surviving. Grieving a changed appearance can coexist with gratitude for treatment working — the two aren't contradictory.

Some patients find it helps to reclaim small amounts of control where possible, such as choosing a headscarf or wig style in advance rather than facing hair loss unprepared, or gradually looking at post-surgical changes with support rather than avoiding mirrors entirely. Others find connecting with other patients who've experienced similar changes reduces the sense of isolation around it.

Intimate relationships can also be affected by body image changes, and it often helps to communicate openly with a partner rather than assuming they see the changes the same way you do — many partners report the changes matter far less to them than patients fear. If body image distress is significantly affecting mood or relationships, oncology social workers or psycho-oncology counselors often have specific experience supporting patients through this.
